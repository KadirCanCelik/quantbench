The lighthouse at the edge of the harbor had been dark for three winters when the new keeper arrived on the afternoon ferry. He carried a single canvas bag, a brass lamp that had belonged to his father, and a letter from the harbor authority that nobody on the island seemed particularly eager to read. The village below the cliff was quiet at that hour. Fishing boats rocked against their moorings, gulls argued over scraps on the quay, and smoke rose in thin gray lines from chimneys that had burned peat for as long as anyone could remember. The old baker, who watched every arrival from her window, noted that the stranger walked like someone who had spent years at sea and was still adjusting to the stillness of land. By evening the whole island knew his name, his age, and the rumor that he had once captained a ship that vanished in a storm. None of it was entirely true, but none of it was entirely false either, and the keeper had learned long ago that a story told often enough becomes a kind of weather: you can complain about it, but you cannot make it stop.
